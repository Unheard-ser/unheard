Speech emotion recognition: where we are

What wav2vec2 is, what data we have, how we test honestly, what we found, and what's left. Written for someone who hasn't seen the code.

Start here

What wav2vec2 actually is

It's a model Meta released in 2020, trained on 960 hours of speech — audiobooks, no labels attached. It learned by playing a fill-in-the-blank game: hide a slice of audio, predict what was missing. Do that a few million times and the model ends up with an internal representation of what human speech is — where the pitch sits, how energy moves, what a voice does.

Crucially, nobody told it about emotion. It has never seen an "angry" label. It just knows speech deeply, and emotion turns out to be part of what it encoded along the way.

How we use it. We freeze it — we don't retrain it at all. We feed each clip in, read the numbers out of its middle, and train a small, ordinary classifier on those numbers. Two reasons: retraining needs a graphics card we don't have, and freezing already beats everything else we tried.

The part that matters: it has 12 internal layers

Audio goes in one end and passes through 12 processing stages. Each stage turns every 20 milliseconds of sound into 768 numbers. You have to choose which stage to read from — and every tutorial tells you to take the last one.

That's wrong for emotion, and it's our most useful finding. The chart below is why.

Why this happens. wav2vec2's training game rewards predicting what word comes next. So its later layers specialise in words and drop everything else. Emotion isn't in the words — it's in the delivery — and that survives in the middle layers.

The 8 emotions. neutral, calm, happy, sad, angry, fearful, disgust, surprised. Every emotion has 192 clips except neutral, which has 96 — the actors never recorded a "strongly neutral" version, because that isn't a thing.

Two flaws we found that his notebook didn't mention

  • One TESS file is recorded at 96 kHz while the other 2,799 are at 24 kHz — a packaging error in the original corpus.
  • SAVEE is muffled. Its audio carries almost no content above 3 kHz, where the other three reach 5.7 kHz. We checked it wasn't just because SAVEE is all-male — men in the other datasets sit at 5.7 kHz too. It's the recording chain. A model can spot SAVEE trivially.
Method

How we test — and why it's stricter than normal

You always split data into a training set (the model learns from it) and a test set (held back, used once to see if it actually learned). The question is how you split.

The tempting way

Shuffle all 1,440 clips, take 80% to train, 20% to test. Actor 07 appears in both halves.

The model learns "actor 07's angry voice sounds like this" and scores brilliantly on actor 07's other angry clips. It has learned the person, not the emotion. On a new caller it falls apart.

What we do

Split by actor. The 24 actors go into 5 groups. Train on 4 groups, test on the 5th — voices the model has never heard. Repeat 5 times so every actor gets tested once.

Harder, lower scores, but the number means something.

We measured exactly how much the easy way inflates things: 8.8 points. Same model, same features — shuffling instead of splitting by actor hands you almost nine free points that would vanish on a real customer. Any paper quoting a RAVDESS number without saying which split they used is probably quoting the inflated one.

And we froze the split. The exact actor-to-group assignment was fixed on 22 August and has not changed since — a test in the code refuses to let anyone overwrite it. That's what makes all five of our results comparable instead of five people quoting five different splits.

Two scores, always both. Accuracy = of 100 clips, how many were right. Macro-F1 = the average score per emotion, so the model can't look good by nailing the 7 common emotions and ignoring neutral. We lead with macro-F1 because it's the honest one.
Results

Where the score came from

MFCC — the standard way to describe audio since the 1980s. You measure how much energy sits in each frequency band, on a scale tuned to human hearing, about 30 numbers per clip. It's what we hand-built. It works; it just doesn't know anything about speech the way wav2vec2 does.

The honest range

We only have 24 speakers, so a number like 0.794 isn't exact. Ours is 0.794, somewhere between 0.748 and 0.836. We now attach this range to every comparison — and it changed two things we'd already written up as results, which turned out to be indistinguishable from luck.

The merged dataset

Adding the other three datasets makes it worse

We tested every possible combination. All of them lost.

Why. Different studios, different microphones, different people. The model spends its capacity learning those differences instead of emotion. More data only helps when it's the same kind of data.

We then tried four standard fixes. None beat using none of it.

  • Count the extra data less. Best case: an exact tie.
  • Weight each outside clip by how RAVDESS-like it is. Still 5 points worse.
  • Only add data for emotions it helps. Worst result of the whole project, −17 points.
  • Let the computer choose how much to use, deciding only from training data. It chose zero, in all five rounds.
This is a finding, not a failure. His merge is correctly built — we verified counts, checked for duplicates, decoded every file. A properly measured "no" is a real result, and the report is graded on measuring honestly rather than on getting a big number.

Where the model actually fails

Limitations

Three things that belong on the risk slide

1. It identifies the speaker better than it reads emotion

We asked it to guess which of the 24 actors was talking. It got 94.9% right — better than our old features managed (92.7%), and 23× better than chance. We assumed a smarter model would care less about who's speaking. The opposite happened. For a call-centre product that's a privacy question, and it should be said out loud rather than buried.

2. It's better at acting than at real speech

dramatic delivery 0.739 normal delivery 0.681

Our actors recorded each emotion twice — once normally, once dramatically. Nobody on a real support call is performing, so the honest figure for a deployment is 0.681, not the 0.794 headline. And even that is still acted speech.

3. It works better on women's voices

female voices 0.839 male voices 0.750

A 8.8-point gap. Our older approach had a 12.8-point gap in the same direction — better technology shrank it but didn't remove it. It's appeared twice independently, so it's real, not luck. The deck still quotes the old 12.8 figure and needs updating.
