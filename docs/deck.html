ISB AMPBA · Foundation Project · Speech Emotion Recognition

Can a machine hear how you feel?

We built one that can — about 64% of the time. The more interesting result is that we can show why most published numbers on this problem are too high, and by exactly how much.

RAVDESS · 1,440 clips · 24 actors · 8 emotions 163 automated tests 173 logged experiments
01

What we built, in one minute

A system that listens to about three seconds of speech and guesses which of eight emotions the speaker is expressing: neutral, calm, happy, sad, angry, fearful, disgust, or surprised.

63.9%
Correct on speakers it has never heard
74.9%
What it scores if you test it the easy (wrong) way
12.5%
What pure guessing would get
92.7%
How often our data can identify who is talking
In plain words

Guessing randomly between 8 options gets you right about 1 time in 8, or 12.5%. We get it right about 2 times in 3. That's roughly five times better than chance.

But the number that matters most is the fourth one. Our measurements are so good at recognising individual voices that a carelessly-tested model can score well by memorising people rather than by understanding emotion. That's the trap this project is really about.

The three things that matter most

  • Our honest score is 63.9%. Tested the common (but flawed) way, the identical model scores 74.9%. That 11-point difference is our main finding.
  • Choosing better measurements mattered ~60× more than tuning the model. Improving what the model looks at gained 13.3 points. Fine-tuning the model's settings gained 0.2.
  • Two of our ideas failed, and we're reporting them. A negative result you measured properly is worth more than a good number you can't trust.
02

Key terms

Every piece of jargon used in this deck, defined once.

pp (percentage point)
The plain difference between two percentages. Going from 50% to 64% is a rise of 14 pp. We say "pp" instead of "%" because "a 14% increase" could confusingly mean 50% → 57%.
Accuracy
Out of every 100 clips, how many did we label correctly? Simple, but it can hide failures on rare emotions.
Macro-F1
A fairer score. It grades performance on each emotion separately, then averages. A model that ignores a rare emotion gets punished here but not by accuracy. We always report both.
Feature
One number describing a clip — say, its average pitch. Our model reads 162 features per clip. "Feature extraction" is turning sound into that list of numbers.
Leakage
When information sneaks from the test set into training, making results look better than reality. Our version: the same actor appearing in both.
Fold / cross-validation
We split the data 5 ways. Each "fold" takes a turn being the test set while the other four train. This gives 5 measurements instead of 1, so we can see how much the score wobbles.
Speaker-independent
Our main test: the actors in the test set never appear in training. This mimics reality, where every new caller is a stranger.
Random split
The easy, flawed alternative: shuffle all clips and split randomly, so the same actor lands on both sides. We run it only to measure how much it inflates the score.
MFCC
Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients. The standard compact description of the shape of a voice at one instant — roughly, the position of mouth and throat.
F0 (pitch)
Fundamental frequency: how high or low a voice is, in Hz. A deep voice ~100 Hz; an excited one ~300 Hz.
RMS
Root Mean Square — the standard measure of how loud something is on average.
Arousal
How energised a speaker is, regardless of which emotion. Angry and happy are both high-arousal; sad and calm are both low.
Normalisation
Putting numbers on a common scale so one loud feature can't drown out the rest. Per-speaker normalisation compares each clip to how that person normally sounds.
Confusion matrix
A grid showing what got mistaken for what. Rows are the truth, columns are our guess. A perfect model fills only the diagonal.
Standard deviation (sd)
How much a set of numbers wobbles around their average. Our 5 folds have an sd of ~5 pp, meaning individual fold scores swing by about that much.
Hyperparameter tuning
Trying different model settings to find the best combination. We did it — and it barely helped, which is itself a finding.
03

Why anyone would want this

Our framing is contact-centre quality assurance — the industry that records millions of support calls and can only listen to a sliver of them.

  • A typical contact centre manually reviews 1–2% of calls. The rest is never heard by anyone.
  • If software could flag calls where the customer sounded angry or frustrated, supervisors could review the ones that actually matter.
  • The same signal predicts customer satisfaction scores before the survey comes back — or when no survey is returned at all, which is most of the time.
Why this framing shapes everything

Every caller into a contact centre is someone the system has never heard before. So "does it work on a new voice?" is not an academic nicety — it is the entire commercial question. That single observation is why we tested the way we did, and it's the reason we found what we found.

04

The data we used

RAVDESS — a public research dataset of professional actors performing scripted sentences in different emotions.

1,440
Audio clips, ~3.7 seconds each
24
Actors — 12 male, 12 female
8
Emotions performed by every actor
2
Sentences, spoken by everyone

Every actor performs every emotion, at two intensity levels (normal and strong), saying two fixed sentences, twice each. That gives exactly 60 clips per actor.

Why this one detail matters everywhere

Neutral is only 6.7% of the data. If our model simply never guessed "neutral", accuracy would barely notice — it would still get most other cases right. That is precisely why we report macro-F1 on every single experiment: it grades each emotion separately, so a class we're failing on cannot hide behind the others.

05

Checking the audio before trusting it

Before any modelling, we opened all 1,440 files and measured them. This is the least glamorous phase and it produced two of our most useful decisions.

0
Corrupt or empty files
50.6%
Of the average clip is silence
48 kHz
Recording quality, identical across every file
2.9–5.3s
Clip length range

Finding 1 — half of every clip is silence

Each recording has roughly a second of quiet at the start and another at the end, before and after the actor speaks. Averaged out, 50.6% of the audio is nothing at all.

That matters because our features summarise the whole clip. Averaging over a clip that's half silence drags every measurement toward the same near-zero background, blurring the differences between emotions.

We trim the silence. Later testing proved this was worth 7.4 pp of macro-F1 — the single biggest preprocessing effect we measured.

Finding 2 — the decision we reversed

Standard practice in audio work is to normalise volume: make every clip equally loud so the model isn't distracted by recording levels. We were about to do it. Then we measured where the loudness variation actually comes from.

Loudness driven by emotion19×
Loudness driven by speaker / mic2.9×
The default would have been wrong

Angry speech is about 19 times louder than calm speech. Differences between speakers and microphones account for only about 2.9×. So loudness is mostly telling us which emotion is being expressed — not who is talking.

Normalising volume would have deleted the single most useful signal in the dataset in order to fix a much smaller problem. We didn't normalise, and there's now an automated test that fails if anyone tries to reintroduce it.

06

What the voices actually show

EDA — exploratory data analysis — means studying the data carefully before building anything, so that later choices rest on evidence.

A rule we imposed on ourselves here. These charts use only the training clips — 1,140 clips from 19 actors. The 5 held-out actors were never opened.

That may sound excessively strict. But if we studied all the data first, every later choice — which features to try, which confusions to chase — would be quietly shaped by the answers we're meant to be predicting. Looking at test data biases the researcher, even when no model has been trained yet. We reordered the whole project to make this possible.

The three signals that carry emotion

MeasurementRange across the 8 emotionsPattern
Pitch (F0)168 Hz (calm) → 296 Hz (fearful)Higher when more energised
Loudness (RMS)angry is 10.2× calmLouder when more energised
Speaking speed1.61 s → 2.14 s of speechFaster when more energised
The observation that predicts everything later

All three line up with arousal — how worked-up the speaker is — rather than with the specific emotion.

So if the model can mainly measure "how energised is this person", it will confuse emotions that share an energy level. And that's exactly what happens: sad gets mistaken for calm (both low energy), happy for fearful (both high). We predicted this from the charts before ever seeing a confusion matrix.

One more thing the charts showed

Female actors average 288 Hz of pitch against 178 Hz for male — a 1.62× difference that is larger than any difference between emotions. In other words, the loudest signal in our pitch measurement is who's talking, not what they feel. Hold that thought.

07

The key discovery

If you take one thing from this deck, take this. Below are two pictures of the same data. The only difference is what the colours mean.

These are UMAP projections — a way of squashing 162 numbers per clip down to 2 so they can be plotted. Clips that are similar end up near each other.

The features know who is speaking.
They do not know what is being felt.

Our measurements describe voices, not emotions. A model trained on this and tested on the same people can score brilliantly by simply recognising voices it has heard before — then fail completely on a new caller.

We proved it, rather than just asserting it

We ran an experiment: instead of predicting emotion, train a model to predict which of the 24 actors is speaking. Random guessing would get 1 in 24, or 4.2%.

Identifying the speaker92.7%
Random guessing4.2%
22× better than chance

Our features can identify which of 24 people is speaking 92.7% of the time. To a first approximation, we built a speaker-recognition system and asked it to do emotion recognition.

The fix, and its limit

Per-speaker normalisation — working out each speaker's average voice and subtracting it, so what remains is how this clip differs from how this person usually sounds — cuts speaker identifiability from 92.7% down to 21.2%. That's why it became our single most valuable decision, worth +6.5 pp.

But 21.2% is still five times chance. Some identity survives, which is exactly why our leakage gap narrowed rather than disappearing.

How we split the data (and why it's the most important choice)

To test a model you hide some data, train on the rest, then check. How that hidden portion is chosen decides whether the resulting number means anything.

MethodHow it worksWhat it tells you
Speaker-independent
our main test		Hide 5 entire actors. The model never hears them during training.How it will do on a new caller — the real-world question
Random split
comparison only		Shuffle all 1,440 clips, hide 20% at random. The same actor appears on both sides.How much a careless test inflates the score
Statement holdoutTrain on one sentence, test on the other.Does it read how things are said, or memorise what?
Statement + speaker holdoutBoth the sentence and the speaker are new.Separates the two effects above
Why we froze the split before doing anything

Five people are working on this. If each of us split the data differently, our scores wouldn't be comparable — and nobody would notice, because every number would still look reasonable.

So we generated the split once, saved it to a file, and committed it. The code physically refuses to overwrite that file, and an automated test fails if it ever changes. It's a contract enforced by software rather than by everyone remembering.

An honest wrinkle

24 actors don't divide evenly into 5 groups. We get four groups of 5 actors and one of 4 — so one test fold has 240 clips instead of 300, and the male/female balance runs 40/60 rather than 50/50.

We checked whether a cleverer arrangement would fix it. It can't — a group of 5 actors can never be an even gender split. We also found that using 6 groups instead of 5 makes it dramatically worse (the standard tool produces all-male and all-female groups), which would have wrecked our fairness analysis. So we kept 5 and documented the limitation.

09

Turning sound into numbers

A model can't read an audio file. It needs a fixed list of numbers per clip. Here's exactly how we produce ours.

Step by step

StepWhat happensWhy
1. LoadRead the file, convert to a single channel, resample to 16,000 samples/second16 kHz keeps everything human speech contains, at a third of the cost
2. TrimCut the silence at both endsHalf the clip is silence; averaging over it blurs everything
3. SliceChop into short overlapping windowsA voice changes constantly — one number for 3 seconds would be meaningless
4. DescribeFor each slice compute 20 MFCCs — a compact description of the shape of the voice at that instantRoughly captures the position of mouth and throat
5. Add motionAlso record how those numbers are changing, and how the rate of change is changing (deltas)A frozen snapshot says little about emotion; movement says a lot
6. Add extrasFour more descriptors: brightness, tonal content, spectral spread, and how often the wave crosses zeroEach captures something MFCCs miss
7. SummariseTake the average and the spread of every number across the whole clipTurns a variable-length clip into a fixed list of 162 numbers
8. Normalise per speakerSubtract each speaker's own average voiceLeaves how this clip differs from how this person normally sounds

We tested 19 different versions of this recipe

Rather than assuming any of these steps was right, we changed one at a time and measured, keeping the model fixed so the difference could only come from the features.

ChangeEffect on macro-F1
Per-speaker normalisation+6.53 pp
20 MFCCs instead of 40+3.71 pp
Adding the 4 extra descriptors+2.58 pp
Shorter analysis windows (the "correct" 25 ms)−1.25 pp
Removing the motion/delta features−6.36 pp
Not trimming the silence−7.42 pp

Combined, the winning recipe gained 13.31 pp — using 162 numbers instead of 240. Better and smaller.

A subtlety worth knowing. One change that won on its own — using percentiles instead of averages, worth +3.34 pp — actually lost once per-speaker normalisation was applied. The two were capturing overlapping information. That's why we tested combinations too, not just one change at a time.

10

The setting that was never chosen

A simple question: what window length do the slices use? It could not be answered from the notebook — and the reason turned out to matter.

What we found when we checked

We had never chosen it. The software library we use, librosa, defaults to a 128 millisecond window — and every speech textbook says to use 20–40 ms. We were running at five times the conventional length, purely because we'd never overridden a default.

Why the default is so long

librosa is primarily a music library. In music you want to tell apart notes that are close in pitch, which needs a long window. Speech wants the opposite trade-off — short windows to catch fast changes. So we appeared to have inherited a setting designed for the wrong problem.

So we tested it. It lost.

128 ms — the inherited default0.4980
40 ms0.4867
25 ms — the "correct" value0.4855

Shortening the window made things slightly worse — by 1.25 pp for the textbook 25 ms value.

Why the textbook advice doesn't apply here

The 25 ms convention comes from phoneme recognition — working out which individual speech sound is being made, where a long window smears neighbouring sounds together.

We aren't identifying phonemes. We average every slice across the whole clip anyway, so fine timing detail is discarded immediately — and a longer window gives a more stable estimate to average. For a whole-clip task, the music-oriented default happens to be the better trade.

What actually changed as a result

  • The setting is now written down explicitly in our code instead of being an invisible default, so anyone can see it and question it.
  • We found five more hidden defaults while looking, and made all of them visible too.
  • We added a project rule: every setting that affects the numbers must be stated, never left to a library default.
  • The honest description changed from "we didn't choose it" to "we tested it and kept it deliberately".

One caveat we recorded. This conclusion is tied to averaging over the whole clip. A model that reads the slices as a sequence — like the neural network track — has the opposite requirement and should re-test window length on its own terms.

Choosing a model

We compared seven standard machine-learning methods on identical features, so the only thing that differed was the method itself.

MethodMacro-F1Wobble (sd)In plain words
SVM (RBF)0.63110.051Draws one smooth curved boundary between classes
XGBoost0.59560.062Many small decision trees, each fixing the last one's mistakes
Logistic regression0.59250.050The simplest option — straight-line boundaries
Random forest0.54880.067Hundreds of decision trees voting
Naive Bayes0.50710.043Assumes every feature is independent — usually too simple
K-nearest neighbours0.46020.031Copies the label of the most similar clips it has seen
AdaBoost0.45040.078Like XGBoost but simpler and older
Why the simple method won

We have 162 measurements but only 1,140 clips to learn from. Tree-based methods (XGBoost, random forest) work by splitting on one measurement at a time, and need a lot of examples to find good split points. An SVM draws a single smooth boundary through all 162 dimensions at once, which suits this shape of data much better.

More sophisticated isn't automatically better. It depends on the shape of the problem.

Then we tuned the winner, and it barely helped

The tuning gain is about one twenty-third of the natural wobble in our measurements. It isn't a real improvement. The practical lesson from this project: on a dataset this size, effort belongs on what the model looks at, not on how it draws its boundary.

The results

Tested on new speakers (honest)63.9%
Tested with a random split74.9%
Random guessing12.5%
The headline finding

Same model. Same features. Same code. Only the way we split the data changes — and the score moves by 11 percentage points.

Anyone reporting 74.9% hasn't lied. They've just measured something that doesn't correspond to using the system on a real person.

And tuning made the leakage worse

New speakersRandom splitGap
Before tuning0.63890.72718.82 pp
After tuning0.63890.748610.97 pp

Tuning improved the random-split score by 2.15 pp and left the honest score completely untouched. That's not coincidence. In a random split the tuning process can find settings that exploit speaker-specific quirks, because the same actors sit on both sides. In the speaker-independent split there's nothing there to exploit.

Tuning against a flawed test manufactures improvement that doesn't exist. A tuned random-split number is inflated twice over.

Always quote the wobble. With only 24 actors split into 5 groups, which actors land in the test set matters a lot. Our fold-to-fold standard deviation is 5.09 pp, so the realistic range on our 63.9% is roughly ±7.5 pp. Quoting "63.9%" as a precise figure overstates what this much data can tell you.

Where the model fails

A single headline number hides who and what the system gets wrong. Both matter for deployment.

By emotion

EmotionF1 scoreNotes
disgust0.744Best — distinctive and slow

surprised0.721
calm0.699
happy0.636Often mistaken for fearful
fearful0.544

sad0.435Worst — heavily confused with calm

The three biggest mistakes are sad → calm, happy → fearful and neutral → calm. None is random: the first and third are two low-energy emotions collapsing together; the second is a high-energy pair.

The clearest summary of what it learned

62% of all mistakes stay inside the same energy band, against 43% you'd expect by chance. The model reliably works out how energised the speaker is — then struggles to say which emotion at that energy level.

By gender — a fairness problem we have to disclose

Female speakers0.6923
Male speakers0.5646

A 12.8 pp gap, worse on male speakers. The dataset is perfectly balanced by gender — 12 actors and 720 clips each — so this is not caused by having less data.

The likely reason is in our EDA: female pitch averages 288 Hz against 178 Hz for male. The emotional pitch movements that carry the signal get squeezed into a narrower band for male voices.

Why we're saying this out loud

A quality-assurance system that is 12.8 pp worse at reading male callers is a genuine deployment risk with a fairness dimension. It's the kind of thing that surfaces publicly after launch if it isn't stated before.

By intensity — where we nearly drew the wrong conclusion

Strong-intensity clips score 8.6 pp higher on accuracy, which looks like clean proof that exaggerated emotion is easier. But macro-F1 is essentially identical (0.600 vs 0.598).

The reason: the strong-intensity set contains no neutral clips at all, because strong neutral was never recorded. So it's a 7-emotion problem that excludes our hardest class, while normal intensity is the full 8. Part of the apparent advantage is an easier question, not easier audio.

Reporting both metrics is what caught this.

14

What we tried that didn't work

Reported in the same detail as the successes. A project that only reports its wins hasn't told you whether to trust it.

Failure 1 — the two-stage "arousal first" model

The idea

Since most mistakes happen between emotions of similar energy, why not do it in two steps? First decide high-energy vs low-energy, then pick the specific emotion within that group. Each specialist gets an easier job.

The result

It scored 1.62 pp worse than the simple one-step model.

Why it failed

The first stage is only 85% accurate, and its mistakes can't be recovered. If a sad clip is wrongly sent to the "high energy" specialist, that specialist doesn't even have "sad" as an option — the right answer is impossible from that point on. About 15% of clips were doomed before the second stage began. The one-step model can hedge across the boundary; hard routing throws that ability away.

What we'd do differently

Use soft routing — let both specialists vote, weighted by how confident the first stage is — so a wrong guess at stage one isn't fatal. Not yet tested.

Failure 2 — the "correct" 25 ms window

The idea

Every speech textbook says use 20–40 ms windows. We'd been using 128 ms by accident. Fixing it should help.

The result

1.25 pp worse. The accidental default was better.

Why it failed

The textbook rule is for identifying individual speech sounds. We summarise the whole clip, so fine timing is discarded anyway — and a longer window gives a steadier measurement to average.

What we'd do differently

Re-test it for any model that reads the slices as a sequence rather than averaging them, where the trade-off reverses.

Partly wrong — our intensity hypothesis

The idea

Strongly-acted emotion should be much easier to classify.

The result

True on accuracy (+8.6 pp), but not on macro-F1 (+0.2 pp). Most of the apparent gap was because the strong set excludes neutral, our hardest class.

What we'd do differently

Compare only the 7 emotions present in both sets, so the comparison is like-for-like.

Every significant decision, and how we'd improve it

The choices that shaped this project — what we did, why, and what we'd change with more time.

DecisionWhy we did itHow we'd improve it
Test on unseen speakers Every real caller is a stranger. Any other test answers a question nobody asked. Get more actors. 24 is few — it's why our scores wobble ±7.5 pp. Combine RAVDESS with CREMA-D or IEMOCAP for a wider speaker pool.
Freeze the split before modelling Five people's numbers must be comparable, and nobody would notice if they quietly weren't. Add a held-out final test set that nobody touches until the very end, on top of cross-validation.
Do EDA after splitting, not before Studying test data biases the researcher even when no model is trained. Keep it. This cost us nothing and removes a whole class of doubt.
Don't normalise volume Measured: loudness varies 19× by emotion but only 2.9× by speaker. It's signal, not noise. Test a middle ground — normalise only the speaker-specific portion, keeping the emotional part.
Trim silence Half of each clip is silence and it dilutes every average. Tune the trimming threshold per clip; 13 clips have very little silence and may be over-trimmed.
Per-speaker normalisation Biggest single gain (+6.5 pp). Cuts speaker identifiability from 92.7% to 21.2%. It needs several clips per speaker to work — fine here, harder on a first-time caller. Test how few clips it can work with.
Keep the 128 ms window Tested the textbook 25 ms and it lost by 1.25 pp. Re-test for sequence models, where the trade-off reverses.
Report macro-F1 alongside accuracy Neutral is 6.7% of data; accuracy alone would hide failing on it entirely. Add per-class recall targets — for a QA product, missing an angry call matters more than mislabelling a calm one.
Pool results across folds Our folds are unequal (240 vs 300 clips), so averaging the five scores over-weights the small one. Keep it, and always publish the fold-by-fold spread beside it.
Test 7 models with default settings Establishes a floor that any complex model must beat to justify itself. Add a simple neural network and pretrained speech embeddings (wav2vec2) — the most likely source of real gains.
Log every run to one file A result that isn't logged doesn't exist. 173 runs, one format, all comparable. Add the code version to each row so a result can be traced to the exact code that produced it.
Write 163 automated tests They catch mistakes we'd otherwise ship — including one where our own scoring code reported a meaningless number. Add a test that fails if any published figure in the docs drifts from what the code now produces.
16

Limitations, and what comes next

Limitations worth stating plainly

LimitationWhy it matters
These are actors, not real peopleRAVDESS is performed, scripted and studio-recorded. Real emotion is subtler and messier. Expect meaningfully below 63.9% on genuine call audio. This is our biggest untested risk.
Only 24 speakersScores swing ±7.5 pp depending on which actors land in the test set.
Per-speaker normalisation needs historyIt uses several clips from a speaker. A brand-new caller with one short utterance doesn't have that yet.
Neutral remains weak (F1 0.540)Real conversation is mostly neutral, so this is the binding constraint on any deployment.
Two sentences onlyEveryone says the same two lines. Real speech has unlimited content.

What we'd do with another month

  • Pretrained speech models. Models like wav2vec2 have already learned what speech sounds like from thousands of hours of audio. Using their internal representation instead of our hand-built features is the single most likely source of a real jump.
  • More speakers. Merging RAVDESS with other emotion datasets would widen the speaker pool and shrink the wobble.
  • Test on spontaneous speech. The acted-vs-real gap is the biggest unknown; even a small sample of genuine call audio would tell us a lot.
  • Soft-routed arousal model. Our two-stage idea failed on hard routing. The soft version is a small change and is untested.
  • Close the gender gap. Try pitch-normalising per speaker so male and female voices occupy comparable ranges before the model sees them.
How to describe this project in one sentence

"We built an emotion classifier that gets 64% on speakers it has never heard, and — more usefully — we can show exactly how much the standard way of testing overstates that, and why."
